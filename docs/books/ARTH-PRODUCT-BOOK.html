Chapter 01

What Arth is

Arth is the system that makes a car dealership answerable for every enquiry it receives, every promise it makes, and every rupee it spends winning a customer.

A dealer group buys advertising, receives enquiries, works them across several departments, sells cars, services them, renews their insurance, and takes them back in part exchange. Today each of those steps lives in a different system or in no system at all, and nobody in the building can answer the two questions that matter most: what happened to that enquiry, and what did that customer cost us.

Arth answers both, and it answers them with evidence rather than assertion.

Every enquiry has an owner and a clock. Every promise has a name against it. Every rupee of advertising can be traced to a delivered car.

The name

अर्थ is one of the four Puruṣārthas: purpose, and wealth legitimately earned. Both meanings are load-bearing. The product exists to give a dealership's activity a purpose it can see, and to make the money it earns traceable to the work that earned it.

The mark is the word arth in lowercase, hanging from a brass rule. Devanagari hangs its letters from a line; an accounting ledger rules a line before a total. We overline where everyone else underlines.

The one-sentence test

If a dealer principal asks what this is, the answer is not a CRM. It is:

The answer "It is the system that tells you which enquiries nobody called, which promises you broke, and which advertising actually sold a car."

Every one of those three is a thing he currently cannot find out, and every one of them costs him money this month.

The five failures

1 · The enquiry nobody called

A quarter of enquiries at a typical group are never contacted. Not badly contacted. Never contacted. They arrive after hours, or during a shift change, or they are assigned to somebody on leave, and no mechanism notices. At an average margin per model, 440 uncontacted enquiries is roughly ₹39 lakh of business that never had a conversation.

2 · The lead cost that means nothing

Every dealer knows his cost per lead. Almost none knows his cost per booking, and the two point in opposite directions. In the month we measured, the campaign with the cheapest leads in the entire portfolio at ₹165 produced zero cars. On a cost-per-lead report it was the best campaign of the month.

3 · The promise nobody owns

A delivery date is agreed verbally, typed into nothing, and broken. The customer finds out on the day. Nobody can say who promised it or when it moved, because the date is a field that holds only its current value.

4 · The disposition that is a lie

Before the anti-gaming rules, not interested ran at 31% of all dispositions at this group, almost none of it with a reason. A telecaller protecting his conversion rate marks hard leads dead. The dealership never learns why it loses, because the reason was never recorded and cannot be reconstructed.

5 · The department that cannot see the customer

A service advisor has a customer's car on the lift whose insurance expires in six weeks, whose household has an open enquiry for a new vehicle, and whose service history would settle an exchange valuation to within 3%. He can see none of it, because service, sales and insurance keep separate records of the same person.

The common thread: none of these is a discipline problem, and none is solved by telling people to try harder. Each is a system that cannot see something, and each becomes visible the moment one record is shared and one ledger is kept.

Chapter 04

What Arth is not

A product is defined as much by its refusals. Each of these was considered and declined for a stated reason.

Not a CRM

Every dealer has one and it changed nothing. A CRM stores contacts. Arth enforces accountability, which is a different job with a different measure of success.

Not a DMS

The manufacturer mandates one and it handles inventory, invoicing and warranty. Competing with it is a five-year build against an incumbent the dealer cannot remove.

Not a dashboard

A dashboard reports. It shows a number and stops. Arth surfaces decisions, and a card that has no action is a report and belongs in a table.

Not a marketing tool

It does not create campaigns or design creative. It measures which of them sold a car, which is a harder and more valuable thing.

Not an HR system

It computes what an incentive should be and exports it. It never pays anybody, never touches a bank account, never holds a salary.

Not a telephony platform

It integrates with one. Building call infrastructure is a licensed business with its own regulator.

A product that refuses nothing has no shape, and a product with no shape cannot be sold in a sentence.

Chapter 06

The moat

Arth has exactly one defensible advantage, and it is not the software.

Advito is the dealer's media buyer. Ad spend and delivered cars sit in the same database because the same company controls both ends.

A software vendor can ask a dealer for read access to his Meta account. Some will grant it, most will not, and none will grant the granular creative-level spend needed to compute cost per booking by advert. We do not ask, because we are already inside the ad account. We placed the spend.

What this makes possible that competitors cannot copy

  • Cost per booking by creative, not merely by campaign, which tells a dealer what to stop making, rather than where to spend more.
  • Delivered cars fed back to the platforms as conversion events, so the same budget bids better. We do not only measure the spend; we improve it.
  • Two-year source value. Portal leads cost more to win and are worth less afterwards: fewer services, lower renewal rates. Computing that needs the same customer record two years later and the original spend figure.
The strategic consequence: Arth is worth most to a dealer whose advertising we also run, and worth least to one who buys the software alone. That is a feature, not a limitation. It makes the agency relationship stickier than either product alone, and it is why the two are sold together.

The honest weakness

The moat is commercial, not technical. Another agency in another city could do the same thing. What protects us is being first in this segment with a built product, and the switching cost of a dealership's two years of history living in our database.

Chapter 09

The accountability layer

This is the product. Everything else is a workspace that writes to it.

The ledger

Every meaningful event writes an append-only row: assignment, call attempt, outcome, commitment, stage change, price change, approval, export. Nothing can be edited or deleted, at the database level rather than by convention. Update and delete privileges are revoked from the application role entirely.

This is what makes Arth usable as evidence in a payout dispute or a disciplinary matter. An editable record is worth nothing in either.

The clock

Every enquiry carries a first-response target and every promise carries a deadline. Working hours gate all of it. A lead arriving at 21:40 starts its clock the next working morning, and the delay is recorded against the branch rather than the executive, because penalising a person for being asleep is how a floor learns to distrust a system in its first week.

The anti-gaming rules

Any scoring system will be gamed. These exist because the specific ways are predictable.

The moveWhat stops it
Dial and hang up to inflate call countUnder 20 seconds counts as not connected. No points, and no penalty either
Mark hard leads dead to protect a conversion rateEvery closure demands a specific fact, checked against the recording. Repeated closures are flagged
Cherry-pick the hot listDifficulty weighting. A cold lead is worth 2.5× a hot one
Inflate a subordinate's difficulty bandSystem-computed, never manager-set, and frozen at assignment
Waive penalties selectively for favouritesEvery waiver is recorded with a reason and counted against the manager who granted it
Keep a dead lead alive on paperA callback beyond 14 days requires a reason
The insight behind difficulty weighting On raw points, the executive who takes only easy leads is the best performer on the floor. Weighted for how hard his leads were, he is the worst.

One dealership's figures: Executive B scored 448 raw and 512 weighted. Executive C scored 386 raw and 802 weighted. C had looked mediocre for a year.

The escalation ladder

Four rungs, every one configurable: executive → team leader at 2 hours → branch head at 6 → dealer principal at 24, with a rupee value attached at the top rung, because that is what makes a principal act rather than note.

Chapter 10

The delivery chain

The gap nobody has filled. Every competitor's product stops at the booking, and the booking is where the customer's experience actually begins.

Between a signature and a handover sit twelve steps across three departments, three lanes running in parallel, and four external parties nobody in the dealership controls. The delivery date is the longest path through it, recomputed whenever any step moves.

LaneStepsBlocked by
Finance and insuranceFile submitted · sanction · disbursement · policyThe customer's documents, then the bank
Vehicle and registrationAllocated · dispatched · received · registeredThe manufacturer, transport, the RTO
PreparationAccessories ordered · fitted · pre-delivery check · handoverStock and bay capacity, both ours

The two rules that make it work

The promised date is an event, never a field. Every movement writes a new row. Without this, three things are permanently impossible: counting how often a promise moved, measuring promise accuracy against what was promised at booking, and answering the most-asked question in any delivery dispute: who told the customer the 12th?

Internal and external blocks are different things. An RTO strike is not the branch's fault and must be excluded from its promise accuracy, or the metric is worthless and the branch stops believing every other number too. But the exclusion is itself reported, so a branch marking everything external is visible.

What the customer sees

One page, no login, a signed link that expires at delivery plus thirty days. It names a human. It never shows an internal step name and never hides a slip. Telling a customer the truth on the 14th is survivable; his discovering it on the 18th is not.

Commercially, this is the referral engine. A dealer principal who can show this page to another dealer principal has done our selling for us, and status messages are WhatsApp utility messages, roughly seven times cheaper than marketing messages. Getting that classification right is worth lakhs a year at scale.

Read the table backwards. Festive C3 produced the cheapest leads in the portfolio and not one car. The cheapest real customers came from the workshop, at ₹2,200. And showroom frontage, universally treated as free, cost ₹29,000 a booking.

Two numbers, never merged

Marketing cost per booking is exact and unarguable. Total cost per booking adds salaries and rent and is directional, and every branch manager will argue about the allocation. They stay apart deliberately: the moment rent is allocated, the argument about allocation destroys the credibility of the first number, which was solid.

Two rows that are never configurable

  • Salary and employee cost is never visible below the dealer principal.
  • Product margin and commission are dealer-admin only. An insurance executive gets a ranked recommendation with reasons and no margin figure anywhere, because a purely margin-driven ranking always puts the priciest policy first and the executive loses the customer's trust within a month.

Advito's own access

Three separate surfaces, not one admin panel. Support can fix a dealer's configuration, sees phone numbers masked by default, and cannot see a rupee of pricing. Every entry is time-limited, reason-coded against a ticket, and visible to the dealer in his own audit log. A vendor who can enter a client's data silently should not be trusted with it.

Telephony and WhatsApp are passed through at cost plus 15%. Steady state works out at roughly ₹12,500 to ₹29,500 per rooftop per month.

Why priced on enquiries and not on seats

Per-seat pricing punishes exactly the behaviour the product needs. A dealer who is paying per head will not give the RTO clerk, the accessories desk or the pre-delivery inspector a login, and those are the seats where the delivery promise actually breaks. Charging per enquiry means the dealer's incentive is to put everybody on it, which is also our incentive.

Plans are data, not code. Changing a price never requires a deployment. Existing contracts are pinned to the version they signed, and negotiated rates above 20% need a second approval and are logged.

What we track that most vendors do not

Cost to serve, per dealer covers messages, call minutes, transcription, lookups, storage and support hours. Without it we cannot tell which customers are worth having. One live example: a group on a 20% negotiated discount runs at 3% gross margin. Nobody would have known.

Why he keeps it: the three that compound

  1. His history lives here. After two years the customer records, service histories and attribution chains are the dealership's memory. Leaving means losing it.
  2. Manufacturer reporting stops being manual. Five to eight hours a month of assembling figures from three systems that never quite agree, replaced by a generated report. Unglamorous, and it is the reason a general manager argues to keep the product.
  3. The advertising performs better. Delivered cars fed back as conversion events improve the bidding. Cancelling Arth makes the same ad budget worse.
The churn signal to watch is adoption, not satisfaction. A dealer whose service team has not logged in for nine days is not a support ticket, it is a cancellation in slow motion. Onboarding time is the best predictor we have: a group stalled at step three after four requests will not adopt.
